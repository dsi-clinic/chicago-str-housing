\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Chicago's Shared Housing Ordinance created a unique regulatory laboratory: a building-level, permanent opt-in mechanism that bans short-term rental activity, adopted staggered across census tracts from 2015 through the present. We estimate the causal effect of crossing a meaningful prohibition intensity threshold on monthly long-term rents using the Callaway--Sant'Anna group-time ATT estimator with pre-treatment trend matching, applied to a panel of approximately 110,000 tract-month observations constructed from six public data sources.

Our main finding is that STR prohibitions are associated with positive rent effects in treated census tracts. The baseline two-feature matched CS ATT is $+\$4.60$/month; the five-feature strict matched ATT is $+\$5.46$/month; the binary-treatment sensitivity run yields $+\$6.4$/month, close to the published clinic benchmark. The full-panel CS ATT ($+\$20.65$/month) provides an upper bound under no pre-trend adjustment. Neither estimate constitutes a clean causal identification: the pre-treatment event study reveals a V-shaped departure from parallel trends (max $-\$16$/month at $t = -8$, joint Wald $p < 0.001$), consistent with prohibitions being adopted disproportionately in tracts that were already on faster rent-growth trajectories. Under HonestDiD sensitivity analysis with smoothness bound $M = 2.71$, however, both estimates remain consistent with positive effects throughout the post-period window. The positive sign is robust across $k \in \{1, \ldots, 5\}$ and intensity thresholds $p \in \{0.10, \ldots, 0.50\}$; the matched ATT ranges from \$3.81 to \$9.97 across the full robustness grid.

\paragraph{The selection finding.} An underappreciated but policy-relevant result is the pre-treatment targeting pattern itself. Prohibition activity was concentrated in tracts with higher rents and faster pre-ban appreciation --- the V-shaped pre-period is a signature of this selection, not a design defect. This means that building associations and landlords in the most desirable, highest-demand neighborhoods were the first and most persistent adopters of STR restrictions. The economic rationale is clear: high-density condo towers on the Near North Side lakefront had large shares of units at risk from Airbnb, organized associations with the resources to seek prohibition, and sufficient rental market power that protecting long-term residential tenancy was individually rational. This targeting pattern has distributional implications: if prohibition raises rents in already-appreciating neighborhoods, it benefits incumbent property owners and deters new renters with lower willingness-to-pay, potentially accelerating displacement rather than mitigating it.

\paragraph{Mechanism interpretation.} The positive sign of the estimated rent effect is inconsistent with a pure supply-release channel --- if prohibitions returned units from Airbnb to long-term rental, we would expect increased supply to put downward pressure on rents. Instead, the evidence points toward a demand-signaling channel: STR prohibition signals to prospective long-term tenants that a building or neighborhood is protected from short-term rental disruption, attracting tenants with stronger preferences for residential stability and higher willingness-to-pay for that attribute. This interpretation is supported by the spillover analysis: isolated never-treated tracts (far from any treated tract and therefore uncorrupted by spillovers) show ATTs of $+\$26$/month, well above the headline estimate and consistent with positive spatial demand spillovers that attenuate our control-group comparison. The dose-response relationship between prohibition intensity and ATT magnitude further supports a direct demand-signaling mechanism rather than a pure supply effect.

\paragraph{Policy implications.} For policymakers designing STR regulation with housing affordability goals, our results suggest that building-level prohibition mechanisms may not deliver the intended affordability benefits in the neighborhoods where they are most likely to be adopted. If demand-signaling dominates supply release, prohibitions in high-demand neighborhoods may accelerate rent appreciation rather than moderate it. City administrators should assess whether the prohibition mechanism targets areas where supply constraints are binding and the tenant population is vulnerable to displacement, or whether it primarily serves neighborhoods with organized associations and strong market positions. The evidence of spillover effects also implies that the full policy impact extends beyond the directly prohibited tracts, potentially affecting the surrounding residential market in ways that standard within-tract analysis will miss.

\paragraph{Research agenda.} Several extensions would sharpen these conclusions. First, individual-level tenant data linking residential mobility to building prohibition status would allow a cleaner separation of the supply and demand channels: if prohibition attracts higher-income tenants at the expense of lower-income incumbent tenants, this would be visible in tenure and income flows that rent indices cannot capture. Second, comparing Chicago's building-level prohibition to city-wide bans in New York City (adopted in 2023) would test whether the demand-signaling mechanism operates differently when the restriction is universal versus building-specific. Third, a synthetic DiD or partial identification approach \citep{arkhangelsky2021synthetic} could provide robustness bounds that are more agnostic about the specific functional form of the parallel trends violation, complementing the HonestDiD bounds we report here.
